Why Study CS? Thoughts on LLM-assisted software engineering

Jan 6, 2026

Dear students of Computer Science,

Almost everything everyone is telling you about LLMs is probably wrong. Some tell you it’s gloified autocomplete, others say it will cure cancer or prove the loftiest new theorems. Nine months ago (March ‘25), Anthrophic’s CEO claimed that AI assistants would be writing 90% of our code within three to six months. We have recently seen that he was only a little too ambitious: the winter break has seen an explosion of programmers uncovering the power of Claude Code, surely a cause for talk around the Kombucha dispenser at OpenAI’s offices.

Claude Code reveals a seriously depressing reality that many programmers are now living: the vast bulk of the thing they call productivity has now revealed to be nothing more than Clude in a loop with command-line tools. To early adopters of AI, this is a rather expected leap. But the 2023-era copy-and-pasting into the textbox, manually crafting a prompt, and carefully stitching the result into our codebase feels downright primitive by comparison to modern tools. Now, we can boot up Claude Code and iteratively build up ideas, tests, implementations, and (possibly) a complete, working app. I argue that the TUI-based interfaces of Claude Code (and of course, Codex, etc.) solve one of the main problems inherent to the web-based chatbots: how to meaningfully iterate, and incrementally build larger software artifacts alongside the LLM. A repository, working alongside the UNIX tools, is a perfect example of a domain ripe for disruption in that regard. I think many of us were telling ourselves that the real ingenuity was in the judiciousness we exercised when carefully copying the LLM’s output and integrating it back into our domain: Claude Code shows that doing that for arbitrarily-complex domains is often just dirt simple, as long as the tools can communicate via text.

Now, this halcyon turn of events has left many students wondering: what is the role of software engineers as a profession? And the specific yes/no question: should I still study computer science? As someone who sells computer science for a living, I am obligated to sell the position of “yes,” but we all know that there are plenty of good reasons to argue “no” as well, at least depending on your circumstance. For the sake of this essay, I will assume you lean towards yes, but perhaps with some skepticism. Even if you agree the answer is yes, it’s an important question, because reveals an uncomfortable and systemic issue faced by many CS graduates: many students float by and operate at a level that Claude Code can easily beat, and in the new world order, it will be increasingly hard to get a job. Just so we’re crystal clear, there’s still some code Claude Code can’t write, even though it can certainly write a lot of very useful code. Of course, “there will always be code that Claude can’t write” is, at least in my opinion, not a very convincing argument: it could well be that the amount of code Claude does write will be massively higher by comparison, in fact I believe many of us now agree that is likely, at least in some domains.

Of course, there’s still a role for humans in generating code, for myriad reasons–though the necessity of doing so may become increasingly small (at least in some domains). But CS undergraduates face a real issue: beating Claude Code isn’t very easy, even (and especially) on challenging leetcode problems. This may push some students to conclude that the AI is good enough at reasoning such as to absolve them from developing a deep understanding of the technical details. I argue that this is the wrong perspective to take, and that instead you should switch your efforts away from mindless and repetitive generation, and towards problems which stretch your intellectual capacity by making you feel stuck. To the degree the LLM can help get you unstuck in a way which genuinely builds your mental model (so that you’re in the driver’s seat, not a maybe-true understanding you got from the AI): great, do it.

Holders of $QQQ will loathe this comparison, but in some ways, I see LLM-assisted software engineering as the 2020’s Object Orientation: it offers a vast swath of opportunity to give lesser-trained programmers the ability to write huge amounts of good-enough code, thus enabling us to hire a vast army of folks who never had to deal with the pains of memory management, pointers, etc. Unfortunately, however, this time it’s different; we all know the hiring boom of easy-money days is over. Seeing Claude Code, many of us take a more pessimistic stance: those that can’t add value on top of Claude will be out of a job. On the contrary, we hear the claim that the most skilled among us will use Claude to rise to brilliant, previously-untouched heights, with more rapidity, speed, and independence than ever before. This is of course, not a new idea, many people speak of the bifurcating skill gap. But who are these folks that will rise to the top? I argue that it is the people who have expended serious intellectual energy to exercise their capacity for understanding deep ideas.

I personally believe the purpose of a university class is to stress your intellectual capacity through repeated exposure to increasingly-complex technical ideas which build coherent vision. In doing this, we teach students skills that (for the sake of this essay) can be broadly binned into generation (synthesis) and verification (checking). In many contexts (e.g., debuggin), we build our mental models via iterating between synthesizing a candidate ideas, then carefully check our work, and reflecting upon what to do next. As a student, I found myself to the idea of “compiling” things into my mental representation: I would spend days at the library (I do not do this anymore, my productivity is unfortunately declined) carefully rewriting books in my own words, which taught me the difference between what it meant to deeply grok something, and to posses an LLM-level understanding of it. One thing I remember from those times, however, is how mentally exhausted I felt at the end of the day. The reason I was exhausted is because I was playing the verifier. I learned a hard truth: after reading a sentence of a book, things often make perfect sense, but when you try to explain it to someone else, you realize you have no clue what is going on. When we do it to ourselves we just feel stupid, but if it happens in an environment that encourages rapid failure, we can grow: effective instructors incentivize rapid failure (e.g., by allowing students to rapidly test their solutions against an Autograder). However, in the case of an LLM, failures often manifest in ways which can be confusing and subtle: students may find themselves frustrated to realize that LLM led them into a hallucination. Feeling confused about what to believe can be a very jarring experience–leading many of us to feelings of repulsion when we read some of our old code—but the LLM can further amplify this anxiety; because it is so shockingly capable of solving a problem, we feel utterly paranoid about hearing the music stop, “what will I do if I actually have to figure this part of the code out for myself?”

Working in terms of abstractions is one of the most important and challenging lessons that budding software engineers learn. As an undergraduate myself, I remember feeling something like: “I can write 100-500 line programs, how do I write a 1M-line program?” The answer is that we have to develop abstract reasoning: almost no developer would literally read the entirety of a large software system (such as the whole Linux kernel, etc.), etc. One crucial skill software engineering students learn is how to employ abstraction when they conceptualize a software system: being able to read just enough of the whole system to understand its architecture, and then employing iterative deepening to learn about subsystems (e.g., the memory manager, networking, etc.) in an on-demand fashion. Right now, many students are telling themselves a comfortable lie: that iteratively prompting the LLM is understanding. There are myriad issues here, confirmation bias is a big concern: the tools are often so good, they give us the impression that there is essentially no point in checking their correctness most of the time. This sounds great, until we get stuck, and then realize we actually didn’t understand things nearly as well as necessary to tackle the task at hand.

This is not a new problem, in fact it is the problem that has plagued CS undergraduates since the beginning of time. The main anxiety faced by students is something like: okay, what do I do next? Structured lessons and projects give us at least some guardrails–this is why building our own application seems so much more daunting than coursework. Being stuck is obviously deeply uncomfortable, because the real world has deadlines, value-measuring systems, all of which depend on our work being complete and correct in a timely fashion. No student wants to be stuck, so they work to exit the stuck state as quickly as possible, often latching on to an incorrect hypothesis (“oh wait, this line must be broken…”) to get back to harmony; this is bad. As you become a senior software developer, you learn that being stuck is in fact that default state when you are working on challenging problems. Plenty of reasonably-skilled programmers can type several thousands of lines of reasonably-correct code over a weekend, but–and this might be surprising to many students–many of the most innovative applications simply do not require any more than several thousands of lines of code to implement meaningfully (at least, if you are content to leave a bit of constant-factor performance on the table). In terms of software complexity, massive line-of-code increases almost always represent feature options among a variety of extensions. Compared to the whole of Windows (tens of millions of lines of code), the core NT is significantly smaller (and also excellently written), at a mere handful of millions lines of code (e.g., 500k for memory management). However, it is absolutely possible to build a reasonably complex operating system in just thousands of lines of code; for example, the sel4 microkernel is only 10kloc, and yet enforces the strongest-possible security properties. Thus, the argument I make to you, students, is to recognize is that the mental energy comprising your engagement with courses should not be primarily bottlenecked by generation, instead, it should meaningfully harmonize generation and verification in a way that allows you to build an ever-expanding mental model.

I recall years ago I chatted with a teaching professor about a worrying trend in CS. This was around 2016, and they mentioned that students were increasingly ignoring the “nuts and bolts” of applications to focus only on the flashy bits that were easy to get not-wrong. The specific concern was hackathons: do they encourage students to do something hard and deep that exercises real engineering skill? Or do they incentivize students to focus on startup-pitch-style motivation with an impressive-looking web interfaces backed by fake data? Thus, I surmise that the trend of students avoiding the genuinely-hard parts of the problem to focus on the flashy parts has been occurring since long before the days of AI coding assistants.

I have chatted with some other professors, and one told me they switched their assessment style to “code comprehension” rather than code synthesis. I think this is an excellent way to see the problem, because it stresses that–when generation is cheap–explanations, reconstructing a reason for generated content–becomes the dominating factor in terms of assessing understanding. Now, careful readers will recognize that the above is simply my spin on the situation, and it is based on our current understanding of how well LLMs work: learn to be the verifier, I say. You might ask: in a world where generators are perfect, what role play the verifiers? This is an apt question, and there are several relevant points to consider.

The first is that English language is genuinely fuzzy, and in almost every case, the ways in which humans describe specifications result in contradictory, unrealizable, or intractable results. In a world run by humans, software engineers expend serious effort interfacing with other humans to refine fuzzy conceptual architectures into correct production software. Thus, one might argue that students should strive to be able to communicate at a variety of levels of abstraction, to other humans, for the purposes of working with customers, users, or some human who needs to understand their needs. In fact, many software engineers spend a significant amount of time doing this, and while it seems reasonable to expect that Claude Code could refine an English specification, there is always an intrinsic value to working with another human. The issue with automation is that this may no longer be worth the price: there is still a market for freelance web developers, for example, but it’s been severely diminished by sites like SquareSpace and WordPress.

Website development is one thing, but LLMs take English-based automated code synthesis to the next level, giving us a general-purpose tool to refine hazy specs into mostly-working software. As an example of how we solved this in the before times, consider enterprise software: every business needs some kinda-custom app for running its operations (billing, hours, HR training, etc.). The software is mostly the same, but some portions are different and customizable (for example, a college might want an online database of class schedules). The enterprise software industry swooped in, building behemoths of mix-and-match components which plug together in confusing and arcane ways, backed by highly-paid engineering teams that tailor the enterprise components to meet the customer’s needs.

Then we have the startup industry: code everything in JavaScript, use MongoDB, tons of libraries, and sell as quickly as possible with minimal code. Of the three (webpage/enterprise/startup), I predict startups will be the most impacted by LLMs: it has never been easier to iterate to “something that looks like a passable minimum-viable product.” If the goal is to demonstrate a cool market gap that is tantamount to a JavaScript app, you can now do that very quickly with AI coding assistants. Thus, these startups will generally face extremely stiff competition.

For the case of website generation apps, they occupy an interesting niche: we often want have some editable (e.g., WYSIWYG-style), symbolic representation of content. LLMs cannot yet reach these cases, and I predict that in the coming years, many of the truly useful applications of AI will involve deeply leveraging AI in a way that allows it to be built into the kinds of symbolic workflows humans love in things like Keynote and Premiere Pro. For example, Adobe Photoshop’s “generative fill” produces cool raster right images right now, but it doesn’t do so in a manner that gives you a layered, element-by-element breakdown: this is the thing all of us obviously actually want, because it lets us refine the results of the AI, and interact with it, just like Claude Code did for our repos. Last, in the case of enterprise apps: organizational bloat, vender lock-in, sales play a huge role in the momentum of those settings, and they will surely outlive the at least the early days of LLM coding assistants.

Thus, to aspiring computer scientists, I urge you to seriously consider how AI will impact your career. But I also urge you to seriously resist the temptation to resort to mindlessly iterating with the AI, toiling in unchecked-guesses because you never bothered to go check the notes or read the book. Of course such students existed before LLMs as well: upon facing broken code, we all have the temptation to temptation to prove it is just a minor typo, we edit the code superficially: sometimes it works, but sometimes it leads us to a significantly more subtle error. The key is being able to check our understanding so that we can incrementally build a mental model which represents genuine understanding: when we fix a bug, we want to know why, to ensure that we haven’t missed a significantly more sinister bug. The issue with this approach is that real complexity does exist, and we deal with genuine complexity is via abstraction: these abstractions necessitate invariants, heuristics, trade-offs, etc. Ideally, you can rapidly leverage the power of modern LLMs to help you reason about these trade-offs: the issue is that you have to be mindful of the fact that the information represents an untrusted guess. One of the biggest problems students have in debugging is getting past a block in their mental model: the code is broken (they have a testcase demonstrating a bug), so they are de-facto wrong (some hypothesis is incorrect), and they struggle to truly believe this. But true growth occurs when they challenge their most basic confusions (“oh, this function isn’t even reached!”), enabling them to get to build a harmonious mental model. As a concrete skill, I suggest developing your instincts for when your understanding of a topic feels discordant.

There is a vast amount of potential in leveraging AI to expand our intellectual capacity, by pushing us to rapidly confront our misunderstands and push past them. However, we often overestimate the impact of change on the fundamental nature of things. At least in your case, a CS student, your goal is rise above regurgitation, mimicry, or rearranging: we all know the parable of the junior engineer who spent all their time trying to rearrange bits and pieces of StackOverflow answers. The goal is thus, as it has always been, to take from our time what we may use to most-optimally execute our vision. In your time as a student, your objective is to learn the key principles of computer science (or whatever field you choose) in a way which maximally stresses your intellectual capacity while offering you the ability to grow from repeated failure; it is of course a coarse classification, and thus throughout your career, I urge you to develop a taste for a specific set of topics in your courses (or computing as a whole) for which you posses enough genuine intellectual curiosity as to be willing to expend the genuine intellectual energy necessary to grok the true essence of things.

– Kristopher Micinski, Syracuse
